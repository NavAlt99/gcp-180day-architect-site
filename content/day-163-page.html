1. Build flash cards for service limits, decision rules and SLAs

Build compact, source-backed prompts for facts that are easy to misremember: service limits, SLA conditions, scopes, regional behavior, quotas, delivery guarantees and decision rules. A flash card must include the source date and the condition that makes the fact applicable.

Problem preview: Symptom: An architect recalls a quota or SLA number but not its region, resource type, eligibility or measurement condition. Effect: The build flash cards for service limits, decision rules and slas decision remains unverified.

2. Practise elimination

Use elimination to remove options that violate hard requirements before comparing the survivors. The goal is not to guess a product from a keyword; it is to prove why an option cannot satisfy the stated boundary.

Problem preview: Symptom: A learner selects a familiar runtime even though the question requires a different network boundary or minimal operations. Effect: The practise elimination decision remains unverified.

3. Watch for keywords without treating them as automatic answers

Keywords compress a requirement but do not choose the service alone. Translate them into a testable decision: “minimum operational overhead” changes the ownership boundary; “lowest cost” requires workload and lifecycle assumptions; “global” still needs data and failure-domain analysis.

Problem preview: Symptom: A learner sees “global” and selects a globally scoped design, missing that data must remain in a region and writes have one owner. Effect: The watch for keywords without treating them as automatic answers decision remains unverified.

4. Time management drills for a long exam

Practise pacing as a reasoning control. Record time per question, flag uncertainty, move on when a question is consuming disproportionate time, and return only when the remaining evidence justifies it. Do not convert a timed drill into a readiness claim.

Problem preview: Symptom: A learner spends several minutes proving an answer to one ambiguous question, then guesses later questions without reading their constraints. Effect: The time management drills for a long exam decision remains unverified.

1. Build flash cards for service limits, decision rules and SLAs: technical mechanics

Do not memorize a bare number. A useful card has service, limit or behavior, scope, unit, condition, source URL/date and what to do when the limit is reached. Separate documented behavior from a local observation and from a design assumption. For SLAs, record eligibility, exclusions, measurement window and service combination; never generalize a percentage across products.

Card format

Question | answer with unit/scope | condition | source/date | design consequence | verification command or page. Delete stale cards when the source changes.

2. Practise elimination: technical mechanics

Mark each option against hard constraints first: identity, data location, connectivity, recovery, managed-versus-self-managed requirement, no-code-change requirement, ordering, latency or cost. Then compare remaining options on operational burden and evidence. Record the exact clause that eliminated each option; a vague “not recommended” is not enough.

3. Watch for keywords without treating them as automatic answers: technical mechanics

Create a two-column habit: keyword/phrase and the requirement it signals. Then write the counter-question. “Least privilege” asks which principal and resource; “without changing code” asks what compatibility boundary exists; “near real time” asks latency, freshness and event semantics; “global” asks write ownership, routing, residency and failover. Keywords narrow the search space; they do not replace the case facts.

4. Time management drills for a long exam: technical mechanics

Use a three-pass approach: first answer clear items, second resolve questions requiring a short calculation or elimination, third review flagged items and select an answer. Preserve a one-line reason for each flag so the review is targeted. Track whether time loss came from reading, indecision, arithmetic, product recall or over-analysis.
