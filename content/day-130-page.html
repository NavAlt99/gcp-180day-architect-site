1. Operating model and service ownership

The operating model assigns decision rights across platform and product teams. Platform engineering owns shared foundations, paved-road interfaces, guardrails, and platform reliability; the product team owns its service behavior, customer SLOs, application data, and first-line diagnosis. Shared ownership must resolve at an explicit interface: a component can have a provider and a consumer, but an incident still needs one accountable service owner. This belongs after the proven baseline because Day 130 packages it as a supported service with an intake, owner, boundary, and escalation path. A request can cross a team boundary without a named decision owner. The resulting delay extends customer impact and obscures follow-up accountability.

Problem preview: A checkout alert reaches the platform team although only the product team can safely disable the faulty feature. Customers continue to receive failed orders while responders negotiate ownership.

2. Change management and release governance

Change management makes risk, authorization, rollout, evidence, and recovery explicit before a change reaches users. Release governance should scale review to risk: a routine, reversible, automated change can use a lightweight standard path, while a high-impact or hard-to-reverse change needs explicit review and a tested backout. Progressive delivery limits blast radius and creates a decision point from observed signals. A change ticket is useful only when linked to an artifact, scope, approver, verification, and rollback owner. This topic turns the Day 129 delivery baseline into a service promise that has supported change paths. A release expands to every user before the health signal is checked. The larger blast radius increases recovery time and the number of affected customers.

Problem preview: A schema change is released to every checkout instance before compatibility is verified. The resulting rollback cannot restore the old application because the data change is irreversible.

3. Incident, problem, and change processes

Incident management restores service through coordinated response; problem management investigates recurring or systemic causes; change management controls the corrective modification. These are related records with distinct goals and clocks. During an incident, assign a commander, operations lead, and communications lead as needed, state known facts, and maintain a current decision log. After stabilization, connect the incident to a problem record and tracked corrective actions. ITIL terms are useful awareness vocabulary, but the specific practice and roles should match the organization’s adopted process. A severity label by itself does not coordinate work. Responders duplicate investigation and customer updates, prolonging service disruption and reducing trust.

Problem preview: Two teams open separate incident threads for the same checkout failure and send conflicting recovery updates. Customers cannot tell whether orders are safe to retry.

4. Runbook automation

A runbook is an executable or operationally precise procedure for a bounded task. Automation can reduce response time and variation, but it also turns operator assumptions into machine actions. Each automated step needs a trigger, identity, preconditions, scope limit, observable result, timeout, retry behavior, failure path, and audit record. Read-only diagnostics are safer candidates for automation than irreversible writes; write actions need authorization and an explicit stop condition. Keep a human approval for actions whose impact is uncertain or broad. This belongs in the service offering because users need an operational response path with known limits. A remediation script retries every failed request without checking whether its prior write succeeded. The retry can repeat a business action and turn a transient timeout into duplicate work.

Problem preview: An automated payment-retry runbook treats a timeout as proof that no order was created. Customers can receive duplicate charges or fulfillment.

5. Service catalog and self-service provisioning

A service catalog presents supported offerings with purpose, owner, service boundary, approved inputs, provisioning path, support model, lifecycle, and evidence. Self-service shifts request execution closer to users while platform teams encode safe defaults and constraints in templates, policy, permissions, and validation. A catalog listing is discovery and governance; it does not itself guarantee the deployed resource is secure, supported, or cleaned up. Link each entry to a versioned template or pipeline, an owning team, support contacts, and lifecycle guidance. This packages the proven baseline as the day's practice. A developer chooses an unsupported region or oversized shape because the offering description omits constraints. The resulting deployment raises cost and support effort while weakening compliance.

Problem preview: A self-service request accepts a region and machine size outside the tested baseline. The platform team cannot support the resulting workload and the product team inherits an avoidable cost or compliance issue.

1. Operating model and service ownership: technical mechanics

A useful responsibility model names one accountable owner per service outcome and separates it from contributing roles. The platform team owns shared network, identity, deployment primitives, policy, and support interfaces; the product team owns application configuration, data semantics, SLOs, and customer impact. A RACI matrix is a communication contract, not an authorization system: IAM, deployment gates, and on-call access still enforce actual permissions. Keep service boundaries visible in an inventory: service, dependency, tier, owner, pager, support hours, runbook, change path, and escalation. The trade-off is central consistency versus local autonomy. Central review can reduce unsafe variation but add queue time; self-service can improve flow but must constrain supported inputs and reveal who operates the result. Measure handoff time, ownership completeness, and repeat escalations, not just team names.

Boundary test: for every dependency, record provider owner, consumer owner, and the signal each can observe. If either side cannot inspect the signal or execute the mitigation, document an escalation path. A shared Slack channel does not itself establish accountability.

Architectural evaluation questions

  • Which role owns the customer outcome during a cross-team incident?
  • Which operational signals can the service owner inspect without platform intervention?
  • What is the escalation path outside normal support hours?

Further study: Google Cloud Well-Architected Framework: CloudOps operational readiness and team responsibilities (accessed 2026-09-29).

2. Change management and release governance: technical mechanics

A governed change path connects request → impact and risk assessment → approval where required → immutable build artifact → staged rollout → health evaluation → continue, pause, or rollback → closure and learning. The release owner controls the deployment; the service owner accepts customer risk; the change approver is required only where policy defines it; an emergency path should record retrospective review. Progressive exposure reduces the population at risk but does not make a bad release safe if telemetry is delayed, cohorts are biased, or rollback is untested. A rollback plan must account for database compatibility, external side effects, and configuration state, not only the binary. Prefer backward-compatible expand/migrate/contract schema evolution. Evidence includes change ID, artifact digest, target, approval, rollout timestamps, SLO/error signals, decision, and final revision. The trade-off is review latency versus control: automate low-risk checks and reserve human approval for decisions with meaningful uncertainty or impact.

Exit criteria must be measurable and time-bounded: compare error rate and latency to a stated baseline over a representative window, then stop when a threshold is crossed. Never infer successful release from a green pipeline alone.

Architectural evaluation questions

  • What is the exposure unit and how is it increased?
  • Who can pause or reverse the release?
  • Does rollback include schema, messages, and external side effects?

Further study: Google SRE Workbook: Canarying Releases (accessed 2026-09-29).

3. Incident, problem, and change processes: technical mechanics

The incident record is the live coordination surface: impact, start time, severity rationale, commander, operations and communications roles, timeline, customer updates, decisions, and current status. The incident commander manages coordination and priorities; technical leads investigate and execute approved actions. Closure follows service recovery, while a problem record carries causal analysis and prevention work beyond the response window. A change record governs fixes that alter production. Join the records by stable identifiers so a postmortem action can be traced to an approved change and verification. Avoid equating a status page update with recovery evidence or a closed incident with solved systemic risk. Signals should include customer-facing SLOs and business invariants, such as no duplicate fulfillment after retry. Trade-offs include response structure overhead and the cost of under-coordination; use clear roles at a threshold appropriate to impact and complexity. Keep timelines factual and distinguish observation, inference, and unknowns.

A blameless review asks how system conditions and process design shaped the event, then assigns owners and due dates to improvements. Follow-up without an owner, measurable completion test, and review date is not a control.

Architectural evaluation questions

  • What customer impact and business invariant define recovery?
  • Which record coordinates the live response and which tracks prevention?
  • Can every action be tied to an owner, due date, and verification?

Further study: Google Cloud Well-Architected Framework: Manage incidents and problems (accessed 2026-09-29).

4. Runbook automation: technical mechanics

Model an automation as observe → validate preconditions → authorize → act within a bounded target set → verify invariant → record result or stop. The trigger must be specific and deduplicated; the execution identity should have the minimum permissions for the action; inputs need schema validation and allowlists; retries must be idempotent or gated by state checks. Set a maximum target count and duration, and fail closed when inventory or identity is ambiguous. Separate diagnostic steps from mutating remediation. An operation should emit a correlation ID, input summary without secrets, before/after state, decision, and result. A timeout means outcome unknown, not action failed. Trade-off: automatic recovery can reduce mean time to restore, while an incorrect or overprivileged automation can amplify a fault. Add a dry-run mode and a human checkpoint for high-impact actions.

For order or payment workflows, check an idempotency key or authoritative commit state before replay. The success condition is the business invariant, not merely process exit code zero.

Architectural evaluation questions

  • What exact precondition authorizes the write?
  • How does the runbook behave when action outcome is unknown?
  • What bounded target and business invariant limit impact?

Further study: Google Cloud Well-Architected Framework: Automate and manage change (accessed 2026-09-29).

5. Service catalog and self-service provisioning: technical mechanics

Treat a catalog item as a contract around a provisioning workflow: discover → select supported parameters → validate identity and policy → invoke an approved versioned template/pipeline → emit resource and ownership metadata → hand over runbook and lifecycle path. The product owner defines workload requirements and consumes the service; the platform owner maintains the template, guardrails, and support boundary; the approver handles exceptions. Inputs should be typed, bounded, and defaulted; reject unsupported combinations with a clear reason. Catalog visibility must align with permissions: discoverability is not authorization. Versioning needs a migration/deprecation policy and a way to identify deployed instances. Include support tier, SLO boundary, owner labels, cost tags, data classification, backup expectations, and retirement steps. Trade-off: a narrow paved road simplifies support and compliance but may not fit every workload; exceptions need explicit risk owner, expiry, and migration path.

A catalog record should distinguish what the platform promises from what the workload team remains responsible for. Revalidate links, templates, permissions, supported regions, and lifecycle steps whenever the offering changes.

Architectural evaluation questions

  • Which input fields are supported and how are out-of-range values rejected?
  • Does catalog visibility align with deployment permissions?
  • Who maintains, supports, versions, and retires the offering?

Further study: Google Cloud Service Catalog: create a Terraform configuration (accessed 2026-09-29).

1. Operating model and service ownership · field case

Enterprise scenario: Synthetic Brightloaf case: a checkout configuration rollout raises payment timeouts. The product owner assumes platform operations owns rollback; platform operations can see deployment health but cannot approve a business feature change.

Failure symptoms and impact: The alert is acknowledged but no one pauses the rollout. Orders fail for nine minutes in the tabletop timeline; this is a scenario, not an observed incident.

Constraints: Preserve order integrity, payment reconciliation, and separation of duties. The incident commander coordinates; only the authorized service owner approves application rollback.

Root cause: Potential causal chain: responsibility matrix omits rollback authority → responders interpret platform ownership as application decision authority → product owner is paged late → mitigation decision is delayed. Confirm with the incident timeline and access-control records.

Diagnostic sequence:

  1. Compare alert, acknowledgment, page, decision, and mitigation timestamps; mark observed versus supplied.
  2. Inspect the service inventory for accountable owner, backup, pager, and rollback authority.
  3. Check who had permission and technical ability to pause the rollout; separate authorization from capability.
  4. Test alternative causes: provider status, deployment health, and application error signal.
  5. Name the first handoff without an owner and quantify its duration from recorded timestamps.

Defensible remediation: Assign the checkout service owner responsibility for customer outcome and application rollback, platform team responsibility for deployment controls and shared infrastructure, and an incident commander responsibility for coordination. Add backup contacts and a tested escalation path.

Verification: Tabletop a second alert and confirm the service owner is paged, the commander is named, the permitted rollback action is clear, and a recovery signal is stated before the exercise closes.

Residual risk: A matrix can become stale and does not grant permissions. Review it after team, service, or support changes; test access and paging separately.

2. Change management and release governance · field case

Enterprise scenario: Synthetic scenario: Brightloaf prepares release r42 with a database field rename and a checkout service update. The team proposes a one-step full rollout and has no recorded compatibility check.

Failure symptoms and impact: A failure could break old and new application versions during rollout and prevent a simple binary rollback. No real release or outage is asserted.

Constraints: Maintain one fulfillment per order, preserve payment reconciliation, avoid exposing secrets in build logs, and use only an approved change path.

Root cause: Potential cause: release plan omits compatibility and explicit abort checks → application versions overlap against a renamed field → requests fail and binary rollback may still encounter changed schema.

Diagnostic sequence:

  1. Read the change record and identify artifact digest, service, data migration, approver, and rollback owner.
  2. Map old and new application versions to schema reads/writes; mark any incompatible overlap.
  3. Check baseline and canary error/latency windows; do not use a single aggregate green status.
  4. Confirm the abort threshold and who is authorized to pause at 10% exposure.
  5. Separate a rollback of application code from a forward-compatible data recovery action.

Defensible remediation: Use an expand-and-contract migration: add compatible field, deploy code that supports old and new fields, backfill and verify, then remove old field only after all readers/writers move. Release to a small cohort with stated SLO gates and rehearse rollback before expansion.

Verification: The record contains a compatible transition proof, cohort size, two-window abort test, named pause authority, artifact digest, and a rollback or forward-recovery step. Preserve the single-fulfillment invariant.

Residual risk: A canary may not include rare user paths, and a delayed metric can make a gate late. Schema cleanup and external side effects may not be reversible; retain forward recovery and communication steps.

3. Incident, problem, and change processes · field case

Enterprise scenario: Synthetic tabletop: after a checkout timeout alert, responders open two incident threads. Support says retry; the application team has not checked whether timed-out payment requests were committed.

Failure symptoms and impact: A retry could duplicate an order if idempotency state is uncertain, while conflicting updates can increase customer contacts. No production event is claimed.

Constraints: One business fulfillment per order; customer communication must state what is known and what users should do; preserve forensic timeline without assigning blame.

Root cause: Possible causal chain: no canonical incident record or commander → parallel fact collection and unreviewed retry advice → business invariant is unchecked → customer guidance diverges.

Diagnostic sequence:

  1. Choose a canonical incident ID and merge timestamps from both threads without erasing original evidence.
  2. Write the known/unknown list; payment commit status stays unknown until reconciled.
  3. Assign incident commander, operations lead, and communications lead; name technical investigation owners.
  4. Check order idempotency key and payment reconciliation evidence before recommending retry.
  5. After recovery, create linked problem and change actions with owners, due dates, and tests.

Defensible remediation: Declare one coordinated incident record, assign roles, pause unsafe retry guidance until payment state is reconciled, issue an approved customer update, then link the postmortem problem and corrective change records.

Verification: Close only after customer-facing health is stable and payment/order reconciliation proves at most one fulfillment per order. Track each prevention action to a verified change.

Residual risk: Incident command cannot supply missing telemetry or payment truth. Reconciliation can lag; record the uncertainty, customer impact, and next update time.

4. Runbook automation · field case

Enterprise scenario: Synthetic case: a runbook sees a payment timeout and retries the charge endpoint without checking the order idempotency key or payment state.

Failure symptoms and impact: The retry could create duplicate payment or fulfillment; the scenario does not assert that a duplicate occurred.

Constraints: At most one fulfillment per order; no secret values in logs; the automation must stop when payment state cannot be determined.

Root cause: Potential defect: retry policy equates timeout with failure → does not query authoritative payment/order state → repeats a non-idempotent action.

Diagnostic sequence:

  1. Record order ID and correlation ID; never record credentials or payment secrets.
  2. Check the runbook's retry condition and whether it treats timeout as unknown.
  3. Inspect whether the idempotency key is stable across attempts and scoped to the order.
  4. Require reconciliation evidence before any second write; record unknown if evidence is absent.
  5. Check max attempts, timeout, target allowlist, permissions, and audit output.

Defensible remediation: Make the runbook read-only until state reconciliation completes. Reuse a stable idempotency key, query authoritative payment and order state, require an operator checkpoint for ambiguity, and verify one fulfillment after recovery.

Verification: Replay the synthetic timeout decision on paper: if commit state is unknown, expected result is STOP and escalate; if a matching payment and order exist, do not retry; if authoritative state proves no commit, a bounded retry may proceed under approval.

Residual risk: A state query may lag or be unavailable. Automation should stop on stale/ambiguous evidence and hand off rather than assume absence.

5. Service catalog and self-service provisioning · field case

Enterprise scenario: Synthetic case: a Brightloaf developer uses a self-service baseline template, but its entry omits allowed regions, owner labels, support boundary, and retirement workflow.

Failure symptoms and impact: The deployment may be unowned, outside the tested support envelope, or left running after the project ends. No live deployment is asserted.

Constraints: Only the proven baseline is offered; users cannot silently bypass policy; the exit artifact links the IaC/pipeline evidence and names the service owner.

Root cause: Potential chain: catalog entry is only descriptive → parameters are not constrained in workflow → unsupported deployment is accepted → ownership and lifecycle evidence are missing at handoff.

Diagnostic sequence:

  1. Compare catalog description and supported inputs with the actual template schema and pipeline validation.
  2. Check the allowed regions and shapes against the supplied synthetic policy list.
  3. Verify required labels and identify which stage enforces them.
  4. Distinguish catalog visibility from IAM permission to provision.
  5. Trace version, owner, support path, review date, and retirement procedure into the entry.

Defensible remediation: Publish a versioned offering that exposes only approved region/shape choices, enforces required labels and policy in the provisioning workflow, names platform and workload owners, links IaC/pipeline evidence, and states support, review, and retirement responsibilities.

Verification: Reject `europe-west1` and `large`; accept a supported pair such as `asia-south1` plus `small`; confirm required labels, owner, template version, support contact, and 90-day review are present in the catalog record. These are tabletop checks, not live validation.

Residual risk: Catalog metadata can drift from deployed templates and policies. Reconcile entries against versions and inventory; require an explicit exception path with owner and expiry.
