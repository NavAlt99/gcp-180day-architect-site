1 · Topics of the day

SLA versus SLO, launch stage, deprecation notices and service lifecycle

An SLA is a provider's contractual commitment and remedy under stated conditions; an SLO is an objective the service owner chooses and measures for user experience. Launch stage, product-specific terms, release notes, deprecation notices, and lifecycle status describe different risks, so a GA label or provider SLA cannot replace Brightloaf's own reliability target or exit plan.

This topic belongs after Day 32's inventory and support work because each selected service now needs an owner, evidence date, operating assumption, review cadence, migration trigger, and viable alternative.

Problem preview: Brightloaf builds a checkout dependency on a Preview feature and records the parent product's SLA as the application SLO. A feature change leaves the team without a supported fallback, delaying order acceptance and recovery.

2 · Technical discussion of each topic

SLA versus SLO, launch stage, deprecation notices and service lifecycle

Separate the contracts. The provider SLA applies only to the named service/configuration and defines eligibility and remedies; it does not promise that Brightloaf's end-to-end checkout meets the same percentage. Brightloaf owns its SLI definition, SLO target, alerting, dependency budget, and response. Confirm the exact product SLA rather than inheriting a number from a product family or architecture diagram.

Gate the lifecycle. Google describes Preview as testing-oriented without SLA or technical-support commitments unless stated otherwise; GA is production-ready and covered by an SLA where applicable; Deprecated means scheduled shutdown/removal. Pre-GA functionality is excluded from the general deprecation policy cited by Google Cloud, so the register must capture feature-level stage and terms, not only product name.

3 · Real-world problem and solution for each topic

Example incident: Preview feature without an exit

Supplied facts and impact: Brightloaf's checkout architecture uses a Preview feature on the order-ingress path. The decision record names the GA parent product, copies its SLA into the application objective, and has no feature-level stage evidence, owner, migration trigger, or tested alternative. A provider notice changes the feature's operating assumption, and the release team cannot safely proceed.

Constraints and evidence: Do not claim an SLA remedy or production support for the Preview feature; preserve checkout correctness and do not shift traffic to an untested replacement. Evidence includes the dated feature page, applicable terms, architecture dependency map, notice, and the incomplete decision record.

Diagnostic sequence: (1) identify the exact feature/configuration, not only its product; (2) retrieve current launch stage, applicable SLA and terms; (3) distinguish Brightloaf's SLI/SLO from provider commitments; (4) trace dependent request/data paths; (5) identify the notice deadline or changed condition without inventing one; (6) assign decision authority; (7) test the alternative before cutover.

Root cause and solution: The exact failure is lifecycle approval based on the parent product's GA/SLA instead of feature-level evidence. Update the register with dated sources, an internal SLO, an owner, a review cadence, a trigger tied to stage/notice/support risk, and a rehearsed migration or retirement option.

Verification and residual risk: A reviewer must reproduce each provider source, locate the feature in the dependency map, and inspect evidence from the alternative test. Stage and terms can change; an approved register does not guarantee the replacement meets production performance or correctness.

Example incident: expired sandbox with no accountable owner

Supplied facts and impact: A Brightloaf sandbox resource remains after its experiment. Its project is visible in the hierarchy, but the asset record has no team role, review date, dependency record, or executable expiration workflow; a budget notification has no named responder. The team cannot decide whether deletion is safe, so cost and operational uncertainty continue.

Constraints and evidence: Do not delete from an age label alone, assume an alert caps spend, or infer no dependency from silence. Use the hierarchy, current inventory, billing/budget owner, activity/dependency evidence, and an approval trail; keep personal data out of labels.

Diagnostic sequence: (1) resolve the resource's full name and ancestry; (2) identify technical, service and cost-owner roles; (3) check dependencies and current activity with the owning service; (4) compare creation purpose to review/expiration policy; (5) notify the owner and backup; (6) archive or migrate required state; (7) obtain deletion approval; (8) delete only in an authorized exercise and reconcile inventory and billing later.

Root cause and solution: The failure point is an informational expiration label without owner or workflow. Create a lifecycle control that turns a due review into owned notification, dependency evidence, an approve/extend/retire decision, and post-action verification.

Verification and residual risk: Require owner/backup acknowledgment, dependency evidence, the decision record, and post-action asset reconciliation. Billing data can lag resource deletion, hidden external dependencies can remain, and this tabletop example does not authorize deleting a real resource.

4 · Step-by-step labs for each topic

Exercise 1: Build a two-service lifecycle register

Goal: Review two genuinely chosen services or features and record an owned, dated operating assumption plus a usable exit option for each.

Expected Result: Two complete register rows that distinguish internal SLOs from provider SLAs and identify evidence gaps without guessing.

Mode: local documentation review/tabletop; no cloud account or deployment required.

Preflight: Create day-033-service-lifecycle.csv. Bring Day 32's asset list and escalation sheet. Select two features that appear in your artifacts; if the artifacts contain none, use the supplied fixtures Order API runtime feature and Order data persistence feature and keep exact product/stage fields marked UNKNOWN until researched.

Numbered Exact Execution:

  1. Create columns service_feature,use,criticality,internal_sli,internal_slo,launch_stage,stage_source,exact_sla,sla_source,notice_channel,owner_role,backup_role,cost_owner,review_date,exit_trigger,exit_option,exit_test_evidence,status.
  2. Open the exact feature page and record launch stage plus checked date; do not infer stage from the parent product.
  3. Open the product-specific SLA and applicable terms. Record none found or not applicable instead of borrowing another configuration's commitment.
  4. Write Brightloaf's internal SLI and SLO separately. Mark the tabletop target as a proposed objective, not measured performance.
  5. Define owner roles, review date, notice channel, one objective trigger, a replacement/retirement option, and the evidence required before exit.
  6. Repeat for the second feature, then have another reader reproduce every source link.
Verification with expected output

Expected: exactly two data rows; every provider claim has a dated URL; no internal SLO is labeled as a provider guarantee; every row has an owner role, trigger, option, and test-evidence field.

Troubleshooting

If the product page and SLA disagree, narrow the feature/configuration and keep the field unresolved. If a service is Preview, do not manufacture an SLA. If a notice channel cannot be confirmed, assign an owner to resolve it.

Cleanup

No resources or cost. Keep the register; remove only disposable copies.

Artifact Acceptance: A reviewer can explain the difference between provider SLA and internal SLO and can execute each row's decision path without relying on an unnamed owner.

Exercise 2: Rehearse the expiration decision

Goal: Join hierarchy, inventory, budget, ownership, and dependency evidence into a safe review for one supplied sandbox asset.

Expected Result: An approve/extend/retire decision record that never treats a label or budget alert as an automatic deletion control.

Mode: tabletop with supplied fixture; no real resource is changed.

Preflight: Create day-033-expiration-review.md. Use this fixture: asset=synthetic sandbox Order API runtime; ancestry=organization/folder/sandbox-project; lifecycle=review-due; owner=UNKNOWN; budget responder=UNKNOWN; dependencies=UNKNOWN; data disposition=UNKNOWN.

Numbered Exact Execution:

  1. Copy the fixture and label every item as supplied fact, unresolved evidence, or decision.
  2. Define the evidence source and accountable role for owner, backup, dependencies, activity, data disposition, cost, and approval authority.
  3. Write three branches: extend with next review; migrate/archive with acceptance evidence; retire with reverse-dependency cleanup and inventory reconciliation.
  4. Because the fixture has unresolved owner/dependencies/data, choose HOLD — evidence incomplete. Do not choose deletion.
  5. Resolve one unknown hypothetically and show why it does or does not change the decision; label the result a tabletop prediction.
  6. Append the two lifecycle-register rows from Exercise 1 and name the owner of every remaining gap.
Verification with expected output

Expected decision: HOLD — evidence incomplete. The note lists all three branches, assigns each unknown, and includes a post-action verification boundary even though no action is authorized.

Troubleshooting

If you are tempted to delete because the review date passed, return to dependencies, authority, and data disposition. If a budget alert is your only cost evidence, record its limitation and the billing owner.

Cleanup

No cloud resources were created or changed. Keep the review with the lifecycle register.

Artifact Acceptance: Combine the two-service register and expiration review into the Day 33 exit artifact. It passes only when each service has an owner role, review date, evidence-backed trigger, and exit option.
